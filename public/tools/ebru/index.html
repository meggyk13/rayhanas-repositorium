About this tray

Ebru (the name is usually traced to Persian) was part of the Ottoman book arts by the 1500s: marbled sheets for album pages, borders and bindings, and sometimes as the ground under calligraphy. Battal, plain "stone" ebru made by sprinkling color after color, is the base every other pattern is built on. Combed forms like gelgit (back and forth) and tarak (comb) are drawn through a battal ground.

The physics is the mathematical marbling of Lu, Jaffer and colleagues: every drop, tine and swirl is an exact, area-preserving deformation, so colors never blend. They only stretch, as they do on sized water.1

Ink. Pick a pigment and tap the tray. Float a layer covers the whole tray in drops of that color; float three or four, largest size first, for your own battal. The palette button switches between period and modern colors.

Tools. Drop: tap, or hold to spread. Stylus: drag one needle. Rake: drag five tines. Swirl: hold to turn the water. Mist: drag to spray fine drops. Rings: tap for concentric drops; pull a stylus through the middle for a tulip. Size sets the scale of all of them.

Combs. Pick a direction, set the tine spacing and the pull, then tap a comb to draw it through the whole tray.

Recipes. Choose a pattern, set the four layer colors (layer 1 is the largest drops), and tap Go.

Printing. Done, lift paper lays a sheet on the water and shows the print. Choose shape, layout (full, album page with a ruled center panel, or mounted) and paper color, then press and hold to save. Prints keeps your last six from this visit.

1. Shufang Lu, Aubrey Jaffer, Xiaogang Jin, Hanli Zhao, and Xiaoyang Mao, “Mathematical Marbling,” IEEE Computer Graphics and Applications 32, no. 6 (2012): 26–35, https://doi.org/10.1109/MCG.2011.51.
